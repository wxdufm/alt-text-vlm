Alt text guidelines

Basics
The purpose of the alt text is to provide an equivalent textual alternative for the album artwork to users who cannot see it, in accordance with WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.1.1 (Non-text Content).
The alt text should be concise. Descriptions should not exceed 130 characters.
Start by describing the central focus of the image. Then describe other needed elements.
Additional sentences may describe typography, color palette, artistic style, or other visually significant elements.
The description should be understandable without requiring the listener to see the image.
Do not start the description with “album cover” or similar sentences. Do not include the text “album cover” in the description unless absolutely necessary to describe the image.
The purpose of the description is not to enumerate every visible detail, but to provide a blind or low-vision user with an understanding of the artwork that is functionally equivalent to what a sighted user would reasonably perceive when viewing the cover in the context of a musical release. The level of detail should be sufficient to communicate the artwork's primary visual content, composition, and aesthetic character without becoming exhaustive.

What determines the central focus:
Text won't usually be the central focus unless they occupy a large part of the image or a part of the central design.
The central focus includes elements that occupy a large part of the image. If those elements are usual elements they should be described simply and concisely (for example, just mention what it is, the shape, the color, etc.). Describe other smaller unusual elements, environment / background of the image, text if useful to the general understanding of the image etc.
The central focus can also include unusual elements, objects or shapes.

Context
The image being described is an album cover. The description provided will be used as an alt text to the album cover image. The description should therefore be written with the assumption that the artwork is part of a musical release rather than a standalone photograph or illustration.
Potential use cases include:
Music streaming and playback interfaces
Album review websites
Digital music libraries
Discogs and MusicBrainz integrations
Radio station playlists
Screen reader users browsing music collections
Music archive and preservation projects

What to include
Describe information that contributes to understanding the artwork.
Examples include:
Main subject(s)
Composition and layout
Artistic medium (photograph, painting, collage, illustration, digital art, etc.)
Dominant colors and color palette
Typography and visible text when it is readable and visually significant.
Visual style (minimalist, psychedelic, collage, zine-inspired, geometric, surreal, etc.)
Overall aesthetic impression when it is directly supported by visible visual features
Relationships between major visual elements
Gender of any person if confident of it

What to exclude
Do not include information that cannot reasonably be inferred from the artwork itself.
Examples include:
Musical genre
Quality judgments ("beautiful", "iconic", "bad")
Personal interpretation presented as fact
Hidden symbolism unless visually explicit
Artist biography
Historical background
Record label information
Release date
Information already provided elsewhere through metadata
The model should describe what is seen, not explain what it means.

Specific cases

People

How to describe people
If someone's whole person is the central focus, then describe broader evident features: skin color, clothes, clothes color, etc. Don't waste characters on describing smaller details of the person unless they are unusual details not typically observed in most people.
If someone's face is the central focus of the image then describe the face's most prominent features (such as skin color, facial expression if visually evident/objective). Don't waste characters on describing smaller details of their face unless they are unusual details not typically observed in most people.

Only describe what is visibly present.
Avoid interpreting intent.


Possible Contributions to Lower Confidence: 
ambiguous main visual element: One or more important visual elements cannot be confidently identified or could reasonably be interpreted in multiple ways, making a precise description uncertain.
bad image quality: The image is too low in quality (e.g., blurry, pixelated, grainy, noisy, or poorly lit) to confidently identify important visual details.
cannot read text confidently: Visible text is partially obscured, too small, distorted, or otherwise unreadable, making it impossible to transcribe or describe with confidence.
complex image with mixed media: The artwork combines multiple artistic media or overlapping visual elements (e.g., photography, collage, painting, illustration, typography) that make accurate description difficult.
cultural or historical reference needed: The artwork appears to contain cultural, historical, political, or religious references that may require additional context or human expertise to describe accurately.
highly abstract artwork: The artwork is primarily abstract or non-representational, making it difficult to identify concrete subjects beyond colors, shapes, textures, or composition.
possible person misidentification: The image may contain one or more people, but their identity, appearance, or even whether they are people cannot be determined with confidence.
possible symbolic interpretation: The artwork may contain symbolic or metaphorical imagery whose meaning cannot be described confidently from visible content alone.
sensitive or indecent content: The artwork contains or may contain sensitive content such as nudity, violence, self-harm, hate symbols, or other imagery that requires careful human review.
unclear visual focus: The artwork contains multiple competing visual elements, making it difficult to determine which elements or features should be prioritized in the description.
